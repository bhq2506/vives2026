Module 1

Supply Chain and Supply Chain Management

Before any technology, we need a clear picture of what a supply chain is, what managing it means, and why it is hard.

A question to start

How does a bottle of Coke reach the customer’s hand?

Take one minute: name every company that has to do something before you can buy it.

Write your list first, then open this
Most people name the shop and the factory. Look further back: who makes the bottle or can, where the sugar comes from, who owns the recipe, who drives the truck, who stocks the vending machine. The next diagram organises these answers.

Separate companies

Most bottlers are independent firms. In Belgium the bottler is Coca-Cola Europacific Partners, not The Coca-Cola Company.

Global recipe, local production

Concentrate travels far; the heavy finished drink is produced close to the market where it is sold. A drink is mostly water, so it is cheaper to ship concentrate and add water locally.

The journey continues

Your purchase sends information and money back up the chain, and the empty bottle starts a return journey.

The pointNo single company brings the bottle to you. Each one depends on the others doing their part on time: that network is a supply chain.

What is a supply chain?

The network of organisations that turns raw materials into a product or service in the customer’s hands.

→

Materials and products

Flow downstream, towards the customer. Returns flow back upstream.

Downstream ↓

Information

Flows in both directions: orders, forecasts, stock levels, delivery status.

Both ways ↕

Money

Flows upstream, from the customer back to every supplier in the chain. The customer is the only source of money entering the chain.

Upstream ↑

What is supply chain management?

The coordinated planning and execution of material, information and money flows across companies, to serve the customer at the lowest total cost.

Integrate

Connect purchasing, production, logistics and sales inside the firm, and connect the firm with its suppliers and customers.

Match supply with demand

Have the right product, in the right quantity, at the right place and time.

Manage trade-offs

Balance service against cost: more stock and faster delivery please the customer but cost money.

Logistics vs supply chain management. Logistics optimises the movement and storage of goods, typically within one company. Supply chain management coordinates decisions between companies.

Supply chains compete, not companies

Efficiency

The efficient supply chain

Goal: the lowest possible cost and high use of assets. Suits products with stable, predictable demand, such as staple groceries.

Effectiveness

The responsive supply chain

Goal: speed, flexibility and reliable service. Suits products with uncertain demand and short life cycles, such as fashion.

Strategic fitAdvantage comes from matching the supply chain to the product and the competitive strategy (Fisher, 1997). A company can win on cost, on service, or on a deliberate balance of both.
Check yourself: which chain would a discount supermarket need? A fast-fashion brand?
A discount supermarket sells mostly predictable, low-margin staples, so it needs an efficient chain: full trucks, high stock turns, few product variants. A fast-fashion brand faces uncertain demand and short product lives, so it needs a responsive chain, even at higher cost. Many firms run both: a grocer can be efficient for tinned food and responsive for fresh bread.

Four persistent challenges

Demand uncertainty

Forecasts are always wrong to some degree, and small changes in customer demand are amplified upstream: the bullwhip effect.

Complexity

Thousands of products, many tiers of suppliers and several sales channels must be coordinated at once.

Disruption and risk

Supplier failures, transport delays, extreme weather and geopolitical events interrupt the flow.

Sustainability

Customers and regulators expect lower emissions, less waste and transparency about where products come from.

Module 2

The SCOR Model

The Supply Chain Operations Reference (SCOR) model is a standard way to describe, measure and improve any supply chain. It gives us a shared map and shared metrics.

Where it comes from

Introduced in 1996 by the Supply Chain Council, an industry consortium. Maintained today by the Association for Supply Chain Management (ASCM).

What it contains

Standard processes, performance metrics and good practices, organised in one framework.

Why companies use it

To map a supply chain, compare its performance with others, and find where improvement is needed.

Which version? This workshop uses the classic SCOR model with its five core processes: Plan, Source, Make, Deliver and Return. It is the version most textbooks teach and the easiest to apply in a short exercise. Later versions added a sixth process, Enable, and ASCM’s current release, the SCOR Digital Standard (2022), reorganises the model into seven processes. The logic of the analysis is the same.

Five management processes

PlanBalances demand and supply, and sets the course for all the processes below.
Suppliers
Sourcebuy
Makeproduce
Deliverfulfil orders
Customers
← ReturnThe reverse flow: products coming back from customers, and materials going back to suppliers.
Linking companiesEvery company in the chain has its own five processes: one firm’s Deliver connects to the next firm’s Source.
Apply it: the Coca-Cola bottler through SCOR
Source is the concentrate and packaging it buys; Make is mixing and filling; Deliver is the route to shops and cafés; Return is the empty bottle and crate; Plan decides how much of each drink to produce for next week’s demand.

What each process covers

ProcessScopeTypical decisions
PlanBalancing aggregate demand and supply into a workable planHow much should we forecast, stock and produce?
SourceProcuring goods and services to meet planned or actual demandWhich suppliers, what quantities, and when?
MakeTransforming materials into finished productsWhat do we produce, in which sequence, at what quality?
DeliverOrder management, warehousing, transport and installationHow do we promise, pick, ship and route orders?
ReturnReceiving returned products and sending materials back to suppliersWhat do we accept back, repair, resell or recycle?
Keep your eye on the right-hand column. AI improves a supply chain by improving its decisions.

Linking back to competitive advantageCustomer-facing attributes express effectiveness. Internal-facing attributes express efficiency. No company leads on all five: a strategy states which of them the company must lead on.
Perfect order: delivered complete, on time, undamaged and with correct documentation.
Cash-to-cash cycle time: the time between paying suppliers and being paid by customers.

Module 3

Artificial Intelligence in Business

AI is software that performs tasks which normally require human intelligence. This module covers what it is, its main types, what it does in business, and when it creates competitive advantage.

A general understanding

Traditional software follows rules that people write. Most modern AI is different: it learns patterns from data and uses them to predict, classify, recognise or generate. That makes it useful where rules are hard to write down, for example recognising a defect in a photo or estimating next week’s demand for each product in each shop.

Two consequences follow. First, AI is only as good as the data it learns from. Second, its outputs are probabilities, not certainties: a forecast or a recommendation can be wrong, so people still need to judge when to trust it.

Four types of AI

Rule-based systems

Experts write explicit “if this, then that” rules. Transparent, but unable to handle situations nobody foresaw.

e.g. a fixed reorder rule

Machine learning

Algorithms learn patterns from historical data in order to predict or classify.

e.g. a sales forecast

Deep learning

Neural networks with many layers learn from images, sound and text.

e.g. spotting defects in photos

Generative AI

Large models create new text, images or code in response to a prompt.

e.g. a chat assistant
How they relateDeep learning is a branch of machine learning, and generative AI is built on deep learning. Business applications are narrow AI: each is built for a specific task. Newer is not always better: a simple rule or a classical forecast often beats generative AI on a numerical planning problem.

Five things AI can do

A working classification for this workshop. You will use these five verbs to label every idea in your group work.

Predict

Machine learning estimates what will happen: demand, delays, failures.

a weather app
Perceive

Computer vision and language models read images, sensors, text and speech.

face unlock
Optimise

Algorithms recommend the best decision within given constraints.

a navigation route
Generate

Generative AI drafts, summarises and answers questions in natural language.

a chatbot
Act

Robots and software agents carry out tasks with limited human input.

a robot vacuum

The role of AI in business functions

Marketing and sales

Recommends products to each customer, adjusts prices and predicts who is likely to buy or leave.

Customer service

Answers routine questions at any hour and directs complex cases to the right employee.

Operations and supply chain

Forecasts demand, plans stock and production, inspects quality and routes deliveries. The focus of this workshop.

Finance

Detects unusual transactions, processes invoices automatically and forecasts cash flow.

Human resources

Supports recruitment and workforce planning, and tailors training to each employee. Biased data can lead to unfair decisions here.

Product development

Analyses customer feedback and generates design options that people then test and refine.

Three sources of valueIn every function the value comes from automating routine work, improving decisions, and enabling new offers.

From insight to action

DescribeWhat happened?
PredictWhat will happen?
PrescribeWhat should we do?
AutomateDo it, with human oversight

Each step delegates more of the decision to the system.

When does AI pay off? A three-part test

Frequent decisions

The same decision is taken thousands of times, such as how much of each item to reorder.

Rich data

Past decisions and their outcomes are recorded and accessible.

Real uncertainty

The outcome is hard for a person to foresee, so better prediction has value.

Use this test on your own ideas. If a decision is rare, undocumented or already easy, AI will add little. Supply chains offer many decisions that pass all three.

AI and competitive advantage

A competitive advantage lets a company win customers or earn margins that rivals cannot match. AI can contribute to it in two broad ways, mirroring the efficiency and effectiveness distinction from Module 1:

Efficiency · costs, asset management

Cost advantage

The company serves the same customers with less inventory, waste and labour, and can offer lower prices or earn higher margins.

Effectiveness · reliability, responsiveness, agility

Service advantage

The company delivers more reliably and faster, and adapts sooner than rivals when conditions change.

The catchAn improvement becomes an advantage only if it supports the company’s strategy and rivals cannot copy it easily. Tools that anyone can buy rarely give lasting advantage; a company’s own data, integrated processes and skilled people do.

Module 4

Using SCOR to Find Where AI Improves Supply Chain Excellence

Start from the process and the decision, not from the technology. This prevents the common mistake of looking for somewhere to apply AI.

For every SCOR process, ask three questions

  1. Which decision is difficult today?
  2. Which AI capability fits that decision?
  3. Which SCOR performance metric will improve?

Process by process

Plan: deciding under uncertainty

Demand forecasting and sensing

Machine learning combines sales history with promotions, weather and local events to forecast for each product and location.

Predict

Inventory optimisation

Algorithms set safety stock and reorder quantities for each item and site, instead of one rule for everything.

Optimise

Scenario planning

Simulation and AI assistants let planners test “what if” questions before the sales and operations meeting.

Predict · Generate
SCOR attributes improved: reliability, costs, asset management. Fewer stock-outs, less excess inventory and a shorter cash-to-cash cycle.

Plan is usually where AI delivers value first, because forecasting is frequent, data-rich and uncertain. A better forecast shared along the chain also dampens the bullwhip effect.

Source: knowing your suppliers

Supplier risk monitoring

Language models scan news, financial reports and delivery records to give early warning of supplier problems.

Perceive · Predict

Spend analytics

Machine learning classifies purchasing data to reveal where orders can be consolidated and prices renegotiated.

Predict

Tender and contract support

Generative AI drafts requests for quotation, compares supplier offers and checks contract clauses.

Generate
SCOR attributes improved: agility, costs, reliability. Lower value at risk, lower purchasing cost and more dependable inbound supply.

Most companies know their direct suppliers but not the suppliers behind them. AI helps monitor more suppliers than a purchasing team could follow manually; the buyer remains accountable for the decision.

Make: keeping production reliable

Predictive maintenance

Models trained on sensor data predict when a machine is likely to fail, so it can be serviced before it stops the line.

Predict

Visual quality inspection

Cameras and computer vision detect defects continuously and consistently on the production line.

Perceive

Production scheduling

Optimisation algorithms sequence orders across machines and reschedule when conditions change.

Optimise
SCOR attributes improved: reliability, responsiveness, asset management. Less unplanned downtime, less scrap and rework, and better use of production assets.

For retailers or service companies without factories, read Make as the operation that creates the offer, for example preparing fresh food in a supermarket or configuring a product for a customer.

Deliver: keeping the promise

Order promising and service

Models quote delivery dates the company can keep, and AI assistants answer customers’ questions about their orders.

Predict · Generate

Warehouse automation

Robots and vision systems pick, sort and count goods; machine learning decides where each item is stored.

Act · Perceive

Routing and arrival times

Algorithms plan delivery routes and predict arrival times from traffic, order and vehicle data.

Optimise · Predict
SCOR attributes improved: responsiveness, reliability, costs. Shorter order fulfilment cycle time, more perfect orders and a lower cost to serve.

Deliver runs from receiving the customer’s order to handing over the product, so it is the process customers experience directly. Which of these have you met as an online shopper?

Return: the overlooked process

Returns forecasting

Models estimate which orders are likely to come back, so capacity and stock can be planned.

Predict

Automated grading

Computer vision assesses the condition of each returned item quickly and consistently.

Perceive

Disposition decisions

Algorithms choose the best route for each item: back on the shelf, repair, resale elsewhere or recycling.

Optimise
SCOR attributes improved: costs, asset management, responsiveness. More value recovered from returned goods, less stock tied up in the reverse flow, and faster refunds for customers.

Return matters for sustainability and for online retail, where many purchases are sent back. Remember: the empty Coke bottle is a return flow too.

The argument your group must make: not “AI is useful here”, but “this application improves this metric, which matters for this company’s strategy”.

A critical view · what must be in place first

Data quality

Models learn from historical data. Incomplete or inconsistent records produce unreliable recommendations.

Process integration

A forecast creates value only when purchasing, production and logistics act on it.

People and trust

Planners need the skills to question a recommendation, and the authority to overrule it.

Responsible use

Privacy, bias, transparency and regulation such as the EU AI Act set limits on what may be automated.

For your presentationName at least one risk or precondition for the AI application you recommend. A proposal without limits is not credible. (Later SCOR versions group these conditions under a separate Enable process.)
